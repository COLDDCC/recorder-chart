The first three notes

B, A and G — the whole first lesson

Start at the top of the recorder's first octave. B needs the thumb and one finger; A adds a second; G adds a third. Each step from B to A to G adds one finger, which is why school methods start here.

  • B — thumb and hole 1
  • A — thumb and holes 1, 2
  • G — thumb and holes 1, 2, 3

Find each note on the interactive soprano fingering chart and tap it to hear the pitch before you play it. Then work down the full note table below, or skip straight to the songs.

Every note

All recorder notes: the note chart, letter by letter

Hole numbers run down the instrument: 0 is the thumb hole underneath, then 1 to 7 from the top. A number listed means cover that hole; anything not listed stays open. ø means pinch the thumb — roll it back to leave a narrow crescent — and ½ means cover only the outer hole of a double hole.

The octave numbers here show soprano sounding pitches (C5 upward). A tenor uses the same finger positions and letter names but sounds an octave lower. Use the separate alto or bass chart for F recorders.

How to use them

Reading letter notes

Letter notes are just the note names written in playing order, left to right. They carry pitch but not rhythm, so they work best for tunes you already know how to sing — your ear supplies the timing while the letters supply the fingers.

In the songs below, a · marks the end of a phrase: breathe there. Letters repeated in a row are repeated notes, not held ones. These examples are for soprano recorder. Use the correct fingering chart for your instrument: C and F recorders use different fingerings for the same named pitch. Letters below omit octaves; use the linked Christmas practice pages for explicit octave numbers and timing.

Why these songs work

These short tune excerpts introduce repeated notes, steps and leaps. Some require more than six notes, so check every unfamiliar fingering before playing. Play each note from the chart first, then string them together at a walking pace — faster comes later, accuracy comes first.

Common questions

Questions about recorder notes

What are the notes on a recorder?

A soprano or tenor recorder is written from C upward and covers about two octaves and a note: C, C♯, D, E♭, E, F, F♯, G, G♯, A, B♭, B, then the same names an octave higher. An alto or bass starts on F instead. The table above lists every one with the holes to cover.

Which recorder notes should a beginner learn first?

B, A and G, in that order. B is the thumb plus one finger, A adds a second, G adds a third — so each new note is one more finger down, and three notes are enough to play a real song.

What do the numbers in a recorder fingering mean?

0 is the thumb hole on the back; 1 to 7 are the finger holes from the top down. A number means cover that hole, and anything not listed stays open. ø means pinch the thumb, and ½ means cover only the outer hole of a double hole.

What is the easiest song on the recorder?

Hot Cross Buns. It uses only B, A and G, and the rhythm comes straight from the words, so a total beginner can play it in the first lesson.

Are recorder letter notes the same on an alto?

Use an alto fingering chart for alto recorder. The same named pitch requires different fingerings on soprano and alto; do not reuse soprano finger positions unchanged.

Why does my recorder squeak when I play the notes?

Almost always a leaking hole or too much air. Cover each hole with the flat pad of the finger, and blow slowly and gently — the recorder needs far less breath than people expect. Squeaks in the second octave usually mean the thumb is pinching too wide.
